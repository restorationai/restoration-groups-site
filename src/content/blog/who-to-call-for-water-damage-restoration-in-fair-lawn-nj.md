## Is this a restoration call, or do you need a plumber first?

Call a plumber first if water is actively running from a pipe, valve, or fixture that hasn't been shut off; call a water damage restoration company once the source is stopped, or if you don't know where the water is coming from at all.

Fair Lawn's housing stock makes this distinction come up a lot. Between the 1920s and 1930s Radburn-era homes and the postwar capes and colonials elsewhere in the borough, original supply lines and aging cast-iron drains are common, and they tend to fail without much warning. If a hose or joint is still spraying, the water keeps getting worse until someone physically stops it, which is a plumbing job. Once the source is off, the next question is how contaminated the water is. Clean water from a supply line is a different cleanup than gray water from a washing machine discharge or black water from a sewer backup, also called Category 1, 2, and 3 water under the [IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/). If you're dealing with a backup specifically, our [burst pipe who-to-call guide](/blog/burst-pipe-in-fair-lawn-who-to-call-first/) walks through that scenario in more detail.

## When do you call 911 or the utility company instead?

Call 911 or PSE&G before calling anyone else if you smell gas, see sparking outlets, or have standing water anywhere near an electrical panel.

Those are safety hazards, not cleanup problems, and they take priority every time. Once the property is safe to enter, a restoration crew can start assessing the water damage. If the water came in through a window, roof, or foundation crack during a storm rather than from an internal pipe, the response is similar, though storm-driven intrusion sometimes needs board-up or tarping work done first to stop more water from getting in.

## What happens when you call for water damage restoration?

When you call, expect a short intake conversation covering the water source, how long it's been sitting, and which rooms are affected, followed by a technician heading out to map the damage with a moisture meter.

Have the shutoff location, your insurance carrier's name, and photos of the affected area ready if you've already taken any. On arrival, the crew checks moisture levels behind walls and under flooring (not just what's visible), sets up extraction and drying equipment such as air movers and dehumidifiers, and documents the scope in writing. That documentation matters for Fair Lawn properties especially, since the Passaic River and Saddle River flooding events, including Ida in 2021, generated a lot of claims where thorough records made the difference between a smooth payout and a drawn-out dispute. For a sense of how long that drying and documentation process typically takes, see [how long water damage restoration actually takes](/blog/how-long-does-water-damage-restoration-take/).

## Should you call your insurance company before or after mitigation starts?

Call your insurer after the water is stopped and drying has started, not before, since most policies require you to limit further damage and that record of prompt action supports the claim.

Most homeowners policies cover sudden, accidental water damage, like a burst pipe, but typically exclude gradual leaks and long-term seepage, and a sewer or sump-pump related loss generally needs a separate water backup endorsement to be covered at all (see the [Insurance Information Institute's overview of what standard homeowners policies cover](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance)). Keep receipts, photograph everything before equipment is moved, and don't assume a given loss is covered until you've checked your specific policy.
