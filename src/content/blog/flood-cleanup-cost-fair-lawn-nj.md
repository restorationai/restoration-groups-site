## What Drives the Price Up or Down?

Four factors set most of the bill: square footage, water category, drying days, and what materials got wet. Everything else is a variation on these.

- **Square footage** affected sets the baseline. Extraction and air-mover placement are priced per square foot or per room.
- **Drying days** add up. Air movers, LGR dehumidifiers and air scrubbers are typically billed per unit per day until a moisture meter confirms the structure is dry, usually 3 to 5 days for a contained basement, longer for groundwater intrusion through concrete.
- **Materials affected** matter. Drywall cut to 2 feet above the waterline, soaked insulation, and carpet pad are usually removed rather than dried, since Category 2 and 3 water saturates porous materials faster than it can be safely dried in place.
- **Access and layout** change labor time. A finished basement with built-ins and furniture takes longer to clear and protect than an open slab.

## Does the Type of Water Change the Cost?

Yes. Category 3 water (sewage, river flooding, storm surge) costs more to clean up than Category 1 clean water because more material has to be removed and treated rather than dried. The [ANSI/IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/) sets the categories restoration crews use to scope a job.

A burst supply line or water heater failure starts as Category 1, clean water, but can degrade if it sits or touches contaminants. Groundwater seeping in through a foundation under hydrostatic pressure, which is common after heavy rain along the Saddle River and Passaic River corridors, is Category 2, significant contamination, even though it looks like "just rainwater." River overflow, storm surge, and any water carrying sewage are Category 3 and require antimicrobial treatment, PPE, and near-total removal of porous materials in the affected zone. That's the main reason a river-flooded basement costs more per square foot than a burst-pipe basement of the same size.

## How Do Restoration Companies Bill for Flood Cleanup?

Most restoration companies, including insurance adjusters, price flood jobs using Xactimate, a standardized estimating platform with regional line-item costs for extraction, drying equipment, demolition, and disposal. That's why an itemized Xactimate estimate is usually the fastest route to a claim payment: the adjuster is reading the same pricing structure the company used to build the scope.

A typical line-item scope breaks out square footage of extraction, number of air movers and dehumidifiers per day, antimicrobial application, and disposal of removed materials. Ask for that itemized breakdown before work starts. It's also the document your insurer will want if you're filing a claim.

## Does Homeowners Insurance or Flood Insurance Pay for This?

Standard homeowners insurance generally does not cover flood damage from rising rivers, storm surge, or surface water flowing in from outside; that requires a separate flood insurance policy through the [National Flood Insurance Program](https://www.fema.gov/flood-insurance) or a private flood carrier. Homeowners policies typically do cover sudden, accidental water damage from inside the home, like a burst pipe, but flood is excluded by name in most standard forms.

A sump pump failure or sewer backup sits in its own category too: that's usually covered only if you've added a water backup endorsement to your policy, not under standard coverage. Given how often the Passaic and Saddle River push water into Fair Lawn basements, it's worth checking with your agent or the [New Jersey Department of Banking and Insurance](https://www.nj.gov/dobi/division_consumers/insurance/homeowner.htm) whether your policy includes that endorsement and whether an NFIP policy makes sense for your address. ## Basement Flooding vs Whole-House Flooding: Does Cost Scale Differently?

Costs scale roughly with square footage, but a whole-house flood isn't just a bigger basement bill. Water that reaches the first floor adds flooring, baseboard, and sometimes lower cabinetry to the scope, and drying has to account for multiple levels instead of one contained space.
