## Why Paramus Floods the Way It Does

Much of Paramus was built out in the 1950s and 60s, which means slab foundations and finished basements sitting on drainage systems that were never designed for the rainfall volumes we see now. Hurricane Ida in 2021 showed exactly how exposed that older infrastructure is, when basement flooding hit homes across Bergen County in a matter of hours. In West Paramus and East Paramus, where many of those postwar colonials and split-levels still run original cast-iron drain lines, a clogged or collapsed line backing up during a storm is just as likely to flood a basement as river overflow is. ## Our Flood Damage Restoration Process in Paramus

The first visit is about stopping the damage from spreading, not just pumping out what's visible. We extract standing water, then check behind finished basement walls and under subfloors, because floodwater in a Paramus split-level basement regularly wicks up drywall and into sill plates well past what's visible at floor level. Flood water is treated differently than a clean water leak: anything porous that touched river or ground-sourced water (carpet pad, baseboards, insulation) typically gets removed rather than dried in place, both for drying speed and for contamination reasons. Once the structure is dried out and verified with moisture meters per IICRC S500 drying guidelines, we move into cleaning and deodorizing before anything gets closed back up.

For homes near the Saddle River with repeat flooding history, we pay close attention to crawl spaces and sump pit conditions during the initial assessment, since a sump system that's kept up with a typical storm often can't keep pace with the river-driven groundwater that shows up during a named storm event.
