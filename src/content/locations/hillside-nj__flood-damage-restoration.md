## Why Hillside Properties Flood

Hillside's housing stock runs heavy on 1920s through 1950s colonials, two-families, and postwar capes, most built with finished or semi-finished basements and original galvanized supply lines feeding into cast-iron drains. Those systems are decades past their design life, and they fail in predictable ways: a cracked cast-iron joint backs up into a basement slop sink, or a sump pump wired to an old outlet trips during a storm and the basement fills while the family sleeps upstairs. On the low ground near the Elizabeth River, river overflow and municipal sewer backup add a second flood path entirely, the kind Hurricane Ida made unavoidable for a lot of basements in 2021. Away from the river, the Route 22 retail corridor and the Conant Street industrial area generate a different flavor of flood call: sprinkler discharge, roof leaks over flat commercial roofs, and overnight losses that aren't discovered until a manager unlocks the doors the next morning.

## Our Flood Damage Restoration Process in Hillside

We start with extraction, pulling standing water out of basements and crawl spaces before it has a chance to wick further into framing and subfloor. In homes built before 1950, that often means dealing with plaster walls and original hardwood that hold moisture longer than modern drywall and engineered flooring, so our drying plan accounts for slower release rates rather than pulling equipment too soon. Once the water is out, we run moisture mapping to find where it traveled, because flood water in a Hillside basement rarely stays where it entered; it follows old utility chases and settles under finished walls that look dry on the surface. Antimicrobial treatment and controlled drying follow, with documentation at each stage for the insurance file. On commercial losses along Route 22 or Conant Street, the same process scales up with larger desiccant and extraction equipment suited to open floor plans and higher ceilings.

## Hillside Insurance Coordination

Most Hillside flood claims fall into one of two buckets: a named-storm event where river overflow and sewer backup are involved, or a sudden mechanical failure like a sump pump or supply line. Those get documented differently for a carrier, and the backup-versus-flood distinction matters a lot for homeowners' policies that treat sewer backup as a separate endorsement. We photograph and log the loss as we find it so your adjuster has what they need without you having to translate basement damage into insurance language yourself.

A detail that matters on Winans and Saybrook streets specifically: many of the two-family homes there share a single sewer lateral between units, so a backup on one side of the house can show up as standing water in a shared basement area serving both units.
