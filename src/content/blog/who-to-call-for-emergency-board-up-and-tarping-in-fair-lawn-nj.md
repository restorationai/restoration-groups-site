Board-up and tarping isn't the repair itself. It's the step that closes a structure back up, keeps weather and intruders out, and stops a bad situation from becoming a worse one, before any permanent work can even be scheduled.

## When Do You Actually Need Board-Up and Tarping?

You need board-up and tarping any time an opening in the roof, a wall, or a window leaves the inside of a property exposed to weather, animals, or people who shouldn't be there. That covers more situations than most homeowners expect.

The most common calls: a wind-driven tree limb punches through a roof near River Road or Warren Point during a storm off the Passaic or Saddle River; a kitchen or electrical fire burns through a door or wall and the fire department has already left; a break-in shatters a window or door along Broadway or in Radburn and the frame won't lock anymore. In every case, the property is open to rain, wind, pests, or a second intrusion until someone secures it.

This is strictly a stabilization step. Permanent roofing, siding, or window replacement is a separate job for your roofer or contractor once the structure is dry, safe, and insurance has been looped in.

## Who Do You Call First: Restoration Crew, Police, or the Utility Company?

Call 911 or the utility company first only if there's an active hazard, downed power lines, a gas smell, or an active fire. Everything else, including the boarding and tarping itself, is a restoration call.

If you've just had a break-in, call the police to file a report before anyone touches the broken entry point; insurers typically want that report number on the claim. Once officers have documented the scene, a board-up crew can secure the opening.

If a storm took out a window or opened the roof, there's no police step. The call goes straight to a restoration crew that handles emergency board-up and tarping, since every hour a roof sits open is more water finding its way into insulation, drywall, and framing. After a fire, the fire department usually clears the property before anyone else is allowed back in; once they do, boarding up is often the very first call a homeowner makes, right alongside [what to do in the first 24 hours after a fire](/blog/what-to-do-after-a-house-fire/).

Your insurance company comes after mitigation starts, not before. Most homeowners policies require you to take reasonable steps to prevent further damage, and a documented, professional board-up is exactly that kind of step. Photograph the damage before anything is covered, and keep whatever paperwork the crew provides; the [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/article/settling-insurance-claims-after-a-disaster) notes that prompt documentation is part of what carriers expect during the claims process.

## What Happens When You Call for Board-Up and Tarping?

When you call, expect a few quick questions: what's open (window, door, roof section), what caused it, and whether anyone is still inside the property. Have the address and a rough sense of the opening's size ready; that lets the crew bring the right plywood, tarps, and fasteners instead of guessing.

On arrival, the crew assesses the opening, clears broken glass or debris, and secures plywood over windows and doors or a weighted tarp over a roof section, sealing edges to keep wind from working it loose. For storm and wind damage specifically, that work connects to the broader [storm damage restoration](/services/storm-damage-restoration/) process, and for fire-related openings it ties into [fire damage restoration](/services/fire-damage-restoration/). Photos are typically taken before and after the boarding for the insurance file.
