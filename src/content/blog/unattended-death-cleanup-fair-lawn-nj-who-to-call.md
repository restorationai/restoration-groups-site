## Who is responsible for cleanup after an unattended death?

Whoever has legal authority over the property is responsible for arranging cleanup, typically the next of kin, the estate executor, or the property owner if it's a rental. This is not a job the police, the medical examiner, or the fire department will do; their role ends once the scene investigation is complete and the body has been removed.

In a rental unit in Fair Lawn or a neighboring Bergen County town, the landlord usually arranges and pays for cleanup before the unit can be shown or re-leased, though lease terms and insurance coverage vary. For a single-family home, it typically falls to the family member or executor managing the estate. Either way, the first call is to a trauma cleanup company, not a contractor or a maid service.

## What does unattended death cleanup actually involve?

It involves removing and disposing of any biologically contaminated material, treating the space to the IICRC [S540 standard](https://iicrc.org/s540/) for trauma and crime scene cleanup, and eliminating odor at its source rather than covering it up. This often means more than surface cleaning.

When a death goes unattended for more than a day or two, decomposition fluids can soak through flooring, padding, and subfloor material before anyone notices. Carpet and pad almost always have to come out. Depending on how long the home sat unattended, hardwood, tile grout lines, or even sections of subfloor may need to be removed and replaced, because porous materials that have absorbed fluid can't be fully sanitized. Odor follows the same path: it's not just in the air, it's in the materials, which is why air freshener or a standard deep clean never actually solves it.

## What happens after the medical examiner releases the home?

Once the medical examiner's office releases the property, usually back to the family or the person named on the lease or deed, cleanup can begin, and most jobs start within a day or two of that release. Before that point, the scene is not yours to enter or alter.

Families and landlords often have questions about insurance at this stage. Many homeowners and renters policies include some coverage for crime scene or trauma cleanup, sometimes as a built-in benefit and sometimes as a separate endorsement, so it's worth a call to the insurance carrier before work begins. Coverage terms vary by policy and insurer, so don't assume either way. A documented, itemized scope of work from the cleanup company makes that insurance conversation much easier, whether you're an estate executor settling accounts or a landlord filing a claim.

## Why shouldn't family or a cleaning service handle this themselves?

Family members and standard cleaning crews should not attempt this cleanup because of real exposure risk to bloodborne pathogens and because incomplete cleanup leaves contamination and odor behind in building materials. [OSHA's bloodborne pathogens standard](https://www.osha.gov/bloodborne-pathogens) exists precisely because biological fluids can carry infectious disease, and handling them without proper PPE, containment, and disposal protocols puts untrained people at real risk.

There's also a grief dimension that gets overlooked: asking a family member to scrub the room where a loved one passed is a hard thing to ask of anyone, and it's not necessary. A trained crew in full protective equipment can do this work discreetly, often while the family isn't even on site.
