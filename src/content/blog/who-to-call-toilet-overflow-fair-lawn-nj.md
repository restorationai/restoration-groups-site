## Plumber or Restoration Company: Which Do I Call First?

Call a plumber first when the problem is still mechanical and contained: a stuck flapper, a bad fill valve, or a clogged drain, with water still confined to the bathroom floor. A plumber's job is to stop the water from coming back, not to dry out what's already wet.

Call a restoration company first, or at the same time, when the water has already left the bathroom. That means it reached the hallway, soaked baseboards or the subfloor under the toilet, or showed up as a stain or drip on the ceiling of the room below. In 1920s-30s Radburn homes and the postwar capes common across Fair Lawn, subfloor and cast-iron drain lines are original or close to it, and water finds gaps fast once it's past the wax ring or the base of the bowl.

Either way, the very first move is physical, not a phone call: shut off the supply valve behind the toilet. That stops active flow in seconds and buys time to make the right second call.

## Is Toilet Overflow Water Clean, or Is It Sewage?

It depends on where the water came from, not on how much of it there is. If the overflow is the tank refilling and spilling over the rim before it ever touched the bowl, that's Category 1, a clean supply-line source under the [IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/).

If bowl water overflowed from a clog, that's Category 2 at minimum, even with no visible solid waste. If the backup is coming up from the drain line, beyond the trap, or carries any solid waste, that's Category 3, the same classification as a sewer backup, and it calls for antimicrobial treatment and PPE, not just towels and a fan. ## What Does a Restoration Crew Actually Do on the First Visit?

The first visit is assessment and extraction, not guesswork. A technician uses a moisture meter to map how far the water traveled under the flooring and into the subfloor, since drywall and subfloor can look dry on the surface while still holding water underneath.

From there: standing water gets extracted, affected baseboards or flooring are pulled if they've absorbed water past the point of drying in place, and air movers plus a dehumidifier go in to dry the structure to the [IICRC S500](https://iicrc.org/s500/) standard. If water reached the ceiling of the room below, that ceiling gets checked and documented too; our [ceiling water damage guide](/blog/ceiling-water-damage-repair/) covers when a stained ceiling can be patched versus when it has to come down. Everything gets photographed and logged for the insurance file before any material is removed.

## Will Homeowners Insurance Pay for This?

Most homeowners policies cover sudden, accidental water damage, which typically includes a toilet that overflows once from a mechanical failure like a bad fill valve or a clog. What they typically exclude is water backing up from a sewer or drain line, which needs a separate water backup endorsement, and gradual leaks from long-term neglect.

That distinction matters here: a one-time overflow from a failed part usually falls under standard coverage per the [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance), but water that backed up into the toilet from the drain system is a different coverage question entirely. Documenting the source and the timeline, photos, the shutoff time, what the water looked like, protects the claim either way. No restoration company can promise an outcome on a claim; that decision belongs to the adjuster and the policy language.
