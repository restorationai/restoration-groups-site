Odor removal and deodorization in Elizabeth comes with a wrinkle most cities don't have: the city's housing stock is dominated by early-1900s two- and three-family frame buildings where a kitchen fire, a flooded basement, or a sewage backup on one floor can push smoke, moisture, or sewage odor into shared wall cavities, stairwells, and HVAC chases that touch every unit in the building. Deodorizing one apartment without addressing what's traveled through the structure rarely holds.

## Why Elizabeth Properties See Persistent Odor Problems

A lot of Elizabeth's multifamily housing in Elmora, Midtown, and Peterstown was built before party-wall fire blocking was standard, so smoke particulate and musty moisture odor migrate between floors through balloon-framed stud cavities instead of staying contained to the unit where the loss happened. In Elizabethport and Bayway, the combined sewer system backs up during heavy rain and tidal surge, and that means sewage odor in basements isn't always tied to a single event, it can recur every time the system is overwhelmed. Add the industrial and diesel exhaust drift from the Port Newark-Elizabeth Marine Terminal and the airport corridor, and some ground-floor units deal with a baseline of ambient odor that masks or compounds whatever we're actually treating indoors.

Landlords in these buildings also have a documentation problem: when one riser failure or fire affects three households, each tenant may file separately, or a single owner's policy has to account for damage across multiple units. Odor treatment plans that don't separate out unit-by-unit scope tend to create disputes later.

## Our Odor Removal and Deodorization Process in Elizabeth

We start with a source inspection, because deodorizing without finding what's actually producing the odor just masks it temporarily. In older Elizabeth multifamily buildings that often means pulling baseboards or accessing shared stud bays to check whether smoke residue or moisture has traveled past the unit of origin. Once we've mapped the source, we clean and seal affected porous materials, then move to active deodorization: thermal fogging for smoke odor that's penetrated wood framing and cabinetry, since the fog particle size matches smoke particle behavior and reaches the same cracks and pores smoke did. For sewage-related odor or widespread musty conditions after flooding, we typically run hydroxyl generators instead of ozone in occupied buildings, since hydroxyl treatment doesn't require evacuating the space for days the way ozone does, which matters when neighboring units in a triple-decker still need to be lived in during treatment. Ozone treatment stays in the toolkit for unoccupied units or short, sealed-room applications where off-gassing time isn't a problem.

We test with our own sense of smell at close range and at a distance, and we don't close out a job on a quick surface spray. Odor that reads as gone at 68 degrees can reappear once a building heats up in summer, so we verify across a temperature range where possible before signing off.

## Equipment & Methods We Use for Odor Removal

Beyond fogging and hydroxyl units, we use HEPA air scrubbing to pull particulate out of the air during the job itself, enzymatic treatments on organic odor sources like sewage-affected subfloor, and sealing products on framing members that have absorbed smoke but don't need to be replaced. Which combination we use depends on the odor source, the construction type, and whether the building is occupied during treatment, not a one-size approach.

In Elizabethport basements near the Elizabeth River, we've learned to check for a second, older odor layer underneath whatever triggered the service call: prior storm flooding (including the Ida event) sometimes left sediment and organic residue in block foundation walls and sump pits that keeps releasing a faint musty smell independent of the current issue. Treating only the recent loss and skipping that older layer means the homeowner calls back in a few months thinking the job didn't hold.
