## What drives the cost of emergency tarping up or down?

Roof pitch, square footage, and access are the three biggest cost drivers, with steep or multi-story roofs costing more because of the extra rigging and time required to work safely. A low, simple roof with easy ladder access is the cheapest scenario by far.

Other factors that move the number: the size of the opening (a softball-sized hole costs less to seal than a six-foot limb strike), whether debris has to be cut up and removed before the tarp can go down, and the material used. Heavy-duty reinforced poly tarps rated for wind hold up longer than consumer-grade blue tarps, and crews secure them with furring strips or battens rather than just nails through the tarp, which tears out in the next gust.

Timing matters too. A call placed at 2 a.m. during an active storm costs more than a scheduled next-morning tarp, simply because of crew mobilization. [The ANSI/IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/) also governs how fast water intrusion needs to be addressed once it starts, since Category 1 water from a roof leak can degrade quickly once it sits against insulation and drywall.

## Does insurance cover emergency tarping?

Most standard homeowners policies reimburse emergency tarping as a mitigation expense, as long as the underlying cause (wind, hail, a falling tree) is a covered peril under your policy. Insurers generally expect homeowners to take reasonable steps to prevent further damage, and tarping is exactly that kind of step.

What this usually means in practice: keep your receipt or invoice, take photos of the damage before and after the tarp goes up, and call your carrier to open the claim as soon as it's safe to do so. The [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/article/which-disasters-are-covered-by-homeowners-insurance) notes that wind and falling-object damage are standard covered perils on most policies, while flood and slow, gradual leaks are typically excluded and need separate coverage. If you're unsure whether to file, our [storm damage insurance claim checklist](/blog/storm-damage-insurance-claim-checklist/) walks through what to document before you make the call.

One thing a tarp does NOT do: it doesn't make the roof repair itself a covered expense if the storm damage falls outside your policy's covered perils. Check your declarations page, or ask the [NJ Department of Banking and Insurance](https://www.nj.gov/dobi/division_consumers/insurance/homeowner.htm) if you have questions about what your specific policy covers.

## What happens after the tarp goes on?

Once the tarp is secure, the next steps are inspecting for interior water intrusion, drying out any wet insulation or drywall, and getting a licensed roofer scheduled for the permanent repair. A tarp buys time; it is not the fix.

1. **Interior assessment.** A technician checks the attic and ceiling below the breach for water intrusion, using a moisture meter to map how far moisture has traveled.
2. **Extraction and drying, if needed.** If water already got in before the tarp went up, wet insulation gets removed and air movers or dehumidifiers go in to dry the structure per IICRC S500 guidelines, preventing the mold growth that can start within 24 to 48 hours on wet materials.
3. **Documentation.** Photos, moisture readings, and a written scope get compiled for your insurance adjuster.
4. **Permanent repair scheduling.** The tarp holds until a licensed roofing contractor can make the structural repair. The mitigation crew that tarped your roof typically does not do that roofing work themselves.

Fair Lawn homeowners should also know the difference between tarping and board-up: tarping seals a roof breach against water, while board-up covers broken windows, doors, or wall openings. If your storm event involved both a roof puncture and a shattered window from flying debris, you likely need both services done together.
