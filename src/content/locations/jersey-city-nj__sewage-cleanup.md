## Why Jersey City Properties See Sewage Backups

Jersey City runs on two very different plumbing realities. In Downtown Jersey City, The Heights, and Bergen-Lafayette, much of the housing stock dates to the 1890s through the 1920s, with clay or cast-iron lateral lines that were never built for modern fixture loads and that root-intrude or settle with the clay soils common across this part of Hudson County. When heavy rain overwhelms the combined sewer system, that backup has nowhere to go but back up through the lowest drain, usually a basement floor drain or laundry hookup. At Newport and Exchange Place, the problem is different: high-rise risers and shared stacks mean a single blockage or failed fixture several floors up can send contaminated water cascading down through multiple units before anyone downstairs even knows something's wrong. Storms like Ida showed how fast low-lying blocks near the waterfront and in Greenville can take on water, and a flooded street often means sewer backups follow within hours, not days.

## Our Sewage Cleanup and Sanitization Process in Jersey City

Raw sewage is handled as Category 3 water, following IICRC S500 guidance, which means affected porous materials like carpet padding, baseboards, and often drywall get removed rather than dried in place. We start with containment to keep contaminated water from spreading to unaffected rooms or, in a multi-unit building, unaffected units below or beside the source. Extraction comes next, followed by removal of unsalvageable porous materials, then antimicrobial treatment of framing, subfloor, and any remaining structural surfaces. Air scrubbers and negative air setups run during the sanitization phase, especially important in older Bergen-Lafayette and Greenville basements where ventilation is limited and odor can linger in masonry. Moisture readings get documented throughout, since Jersey City's older foundations often read damp at baseline and we need a clear before-and-after record for the claim file.

## Jersey City Insurance & HOA Coordination

Sewage losses get documented differently depending on who's receiving the report. A landlord in a Journal Square rowhouse conversion typically needs photos, moisture logs, and a scope of work for a single-family claim. A condo association at Newport or Exchange Place usually needs unit-by-unit documentation, since the building's master policy and each owner's individual policy may both come into play, and the board often wants a clear line between what the association's insurer covers in common areas and what falls to the unit owner. We build our paperwork to work either way, labeling affected areas by unit or address so whoever is managing the claim, a property manager, a board treasurer, or a homeowner, has what they need without chasing us for it later.

## Local note

In Heights rowhouses built before 1930, cellar floor drains often tie into the same lateral as the kitchen and bathroom stacks above, so a backup frequently shows up at the lowest point in the house first, even when the actual blockage is further down the line near the street connection. We check that floor drain early in the assessment, because it tells us whether the problem is isolated to the property or whether the city main itself is surcharging, which changes both the cleanup plan and who ultimately needs to be called about the line.
