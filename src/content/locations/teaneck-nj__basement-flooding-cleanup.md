## Why Teaneck Basements Flood

Much of Teaneck's housing stock dates to the 1920s through 1940s, brick Tudors and center-hall colonials with basements that were finished decades after original construction, often without modern waterproofing or sump systems. The cast-iron drain lines common in these homes narrow with age and corrosion, so a heavy storm that would be a non-event in a newer home can push water back up through a floor drain here. Add the township's low-lying stretches along Teaneck Creek and the Hackensack River on the eastern edge, and you get basements that flood from two directions: groundwater rising against an old foundation, and municipal or sewer lines surcharging under storm load. ## Our Basement Flooding Cleanup Process

We start by determining water category, clean groundwater seepage is handled differently than water carrying sewage or runoff contamination, which changes what gets removed versus dried in place. Standing water comes out with submersible pumps and truck-mounted extraction, then we check flooring and wall cavities with moisture meters, because a slab that reads dry on top can still be holding water underneath for days. Drywall and insulation that wicked water above the flood line come out in a clean, defined cut line. Antimicrobial treatment goes down on any surface that had contact with gray or black water, and commercial-grade air movers and dehumidifiers run until moisture readings in the studs and subfloor match the rest of the house, not just until the floor looks dry. For older homes with plaster or masonry basement walls, we extend drying time accordingly since those materials release trapped moisture more slowly than modern drywall.
