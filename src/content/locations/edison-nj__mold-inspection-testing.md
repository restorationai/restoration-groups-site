A finished basement in a Stelton colonial with a 1970s cast-iron drain stack behaves nothing like a crawl space under a Clara Barton cape with original supply lines, and a split-level near Oak Tree with a slow, unnoticed roof leak tells a different story than a Route 1 retail space with a sprinkler head that's been weeping into a drop ceiling for months. Testing has to account for what the building actually is, not just what's visible on the surface.

## Why Edison Properties See Mold Issues

Edison's housing stock is mostly postwar: capes, split-levels and colonials built from the late 1940s through the 1960s, many with finished basements added decades after the original construction. Those basements often sit on original supply plumbing and cast-iron drain lines that are now reaching the end of their service life, and a pinhole leak behind a finished wall can run for weeks before anyone notices a musty smell or a soft spot in the drywall. Low-lying parcels near the Raritan River and the Mill Brook tributaries saw real flooding during Ida in 2021, and basements in those areas are still more prone to lingering moisture in wall cavities and subfloor framing than homes on higher ground. On the commercial side, the Route 1 and Route 27 corridors carry a dense mix of big-box retail, restaurants and warehouse space where roof leaks and overnight sprinkler discharges can sit undiscovered until a tenant notices a smell or discoloration on a ceiling tile. In both settings, visible mold is usually the last sign to show up, not the first.

## Our Mold Inspection and Testing Process in Edison

We start with a visual and moisture survey of the areas most likely to hold hidden water: basement rim joists, behind baseboard in finished lower levels, around bathroom exhaust fans, and near any roof penetration in commercial buildings along the Route 1 corridor. A moisture meter and thermal imaging help us find damp framing and wall cavities before we cut or sample anything. Where conditions warrant it, we collect air samples and, in some cases, surface or tape-lift samples, and send them to a third-party lab for analysis, comparing indoor spore counts to an outdoor baseline reading taken on-site the same day. The lab results tell us what's actually present and at what concentration, which is the difference between a guess and a documented assessment. We write up findings in a report that notes moisture source, affected materials and lab data, something homeowners can hand directly to an insurance adjuster or a property manager without translation.

## Equipment & Methods We Use for Mold Inspection

Because Edison's building stock spans original 1950s construction to newer multifamily complexes near North Edison and Menlo Park, we calibrate equipment to the building type. Thermal cameras read differently on plaster-and-lath walls than on modern drywall, and crawl spaces under older capes often need a different moisture meter setting than a slab-on-grade commercial unit. Our air sampling pumps and spore traps are set per the manufacturer's calibration standard, and all lab analysis goes through an independent, accredited laboratory, not an in-house read, so results hold up if a claim or a real estate transaction depends on them.

Homes within a few blocks of the Raritan River floodplain, including pockets near Nixon, sometimes pass a visual inspection with no obvious staining but still test high for airborne spores, because water that entered during Ida-era flooding migrated into wall cavities and insulation rather than pooling where anyone could see it. We've learned to treat any finished basement in that footprint as a candidate for air sampling even when the walls look clean, since surface appearance and actual spore concentration don't always agree in these older foundations.
