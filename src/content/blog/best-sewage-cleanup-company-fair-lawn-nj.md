## What separates a real sewage cleanup company from a carpet cleaner?

A sewage cleanup company treats the loss as a biohazard under the [ANSI/IICRC S500](https://iicrc.org/s500/) standard, not as a water spill to vacuum up. That means PPE, antimicrobial treatment, and a plan for what gets removed versus dried.

A general carpet or upholstery cleaner is equipped for Category 1 (clean) water, like a burst supply line. Sewage from a backed-up lateral is classified Category 3, the standard's most contaminated tier, and it's treated differently from the first minute on site: contaminated porous materials like carpet pad, baseboard, and often drywall below the waterline get removed rather than dried in place, because drying does not disinfect them.

If a company's answer to "what's your plan for the carpet pad" is "we'll dry it," that's a sign they're not equipped for Category 3 work.

## Why does IICRC S500 Category 3 training actually matter?

It matters because it determines what the crew removes, what PPE they wear, and how they dispose of contaminated material, not just how fast they show up with a fan.

Technicians trained to the S500 standard know to seal off the affected area, wear gloves and respiratory protection appropriate to sewage exposure, and apply an EPA-registered antimicrobial to studs, subfloor, and any surface the water touched before anything gets rebuilt. They also know which materials simply cannot be saved: insulation that's been wet with black water, for instance, almost always has to come out. A crew without that training may dry what should have been removed, which sets up mold and odor problems that show up weeks later.

## What questions should you ask before anyone touches drywall?

Ask these four questions before you let a crew start demolition, because the answers tell you whether you're hiring a biohazard specialist or a general cleaning service.

1. **Are your technicians IICRC certified for Category 3 water, and can I see the firm's certification?**
2. **Will you give me a written scope of work, with photos and moisture readings, before you remove anything?** This is the documentation your insurer will want.
3. **What specifically are you removing versus drying, and why?** A vague answer is a red flag.
4. **How do you dispose of contaminated materials, and does your price include antimicrobial treatment, not just extraction?**

A company that answers all four clearly, in writing, is the one worth hiring. One that bristles at the questions usually isn't equipped for the job.

## Should you call a plumber or a sewage cleanup company first?

Call a plumber first only if the backup is still actively flowing, since stopping the source is a plumbing problem. Once the flow has stopped, a sewage cleanup company handles the extraction, sanitizing, and insurance documentation, which a plumber typically does not do.

## How do you protect your insurance claim on a sewage loss?

You protect the claim by documenting the loss before cleanup starts and understanding that most standard homeowners policies do not cover sewer backup without an added endorsement.

Photograph the standing water and the waterline on walls before anything is touched. Ask the company for a written, itemized scope and moisture logs taken throughout the drying process, which carriers use to verify the extent of the loss. On coverage: a [standard homeowners policy](https://www.iii.org/article/which-disasters-are-covered-by-homeowners-insurance) usually excludes sewer and drain backup unless you've purchased a water backup (sewer/sump) endorsement, so check your declarations page before you assume the loss is covered. Once the sanitizing and drying are done, you'll likely need a separate licensed contractor for drywall, flooring, and any other rebuild work; a mitigation company's job ends at making the space clean and dry, not at putting it back together.

A sewage backup in a finished basement is one of the few restoration calls where the wrong hire genuinely makes the problem worse. Confirm Category 3 certification, get the scope in writing, and treat documentation as part of the job, not an afterthought.
