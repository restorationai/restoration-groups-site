## Why does sewage cleanup cost more than clean water cleanup?

Sewage backup is classified as Category 3 water, the most contaminated category under the [ANSI/IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/), and that classification is what drives the higher cost. Category 3 water contains bacteria, viruses and other pathogens, so far less of the structure can simply be dried and put back into service.

A clean-water loss, like a burst supply line, often lets a crew dry drywall and subfloor in place. A sewage backup usually means cutting out and bagging the materials the water touched, using full PPE, running containment, and applying antimicrobial treatment before anything gets dried. That extra labor, disposal and material cost is the real reason a sewage job runs higher than a comparable clean-water job of the same size, something we also cover in our [basement flooding cost guide](/blog/flood-cleanup-cost-fair-lawn-nj/).

## What has to be removed after a category 3 sewage loss?

Most porous materials that contacted sewage water get removed rather than cleaned, because they can't be reliably sanitized. That typically includes carpet and pad, drywall up to the waterline plus a margin above it, wall insulation, and particleboard cabinetry or baseboards that absorbed water.

Hard, non-porous surfaces, concrete, tile, and solid wood doors or trim, can usually be cleaned and disinfected in place rather than discarded. Once the contaminated material is out and the space is cleaned and treated, the drywall and flooring that were removed need to be replaced by a contractor. That rebuild step is separate from the cleanup and sanitizing work itself.

## Does homeowners insurance cover sewage backup cleanup?

Most standard homeowners policies do not cover sewer or drain backup unless you've added a water backup (sewer/sump) endorsement, a separate add-on most carriers sell for a modest additional premium. Without that endorsement, cleanup after a sewage backup is typically an out-of-pocket cost.

If you do carry the endorsement, it usually comes with its own coverage limit separate from your main dwelling coverage, so check your declarations page for the actual number rather than assuming full coverage. According to the [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/article/which-disasters-are-covered-by-homeowners-insurance), backup and overflow from sewers and drains is one of the most commonly excluded perils on a standard policy. Photographing the damage before anything is removed, and keeping the invoice from cleanup, protects whatever claim you do have.

## How long does sewage cleanup take?

A typical residential sewage backup takes two to five days from the first phone call to a dry, sanitized space, though larger losses with more demolition can run longer. The timeline has four parts: extraction and removal, cleaning and antimicrobial treatment, structural drying, and a final check before rebuild work starts.

1. **Extraction and removal** (day one): standing water is pumped or extracted, contaminated materials are cut out and bagged for disposal.
2. **Cleaning and antimicrobial treatment**: remaining surfaces are cleaned and treated to address bacteria and odor-causing residue.
3. **Structural drying** (typically two to four days): air movers and dehumidifiers run continuously while a technician logs moisture readings until the space reaches a dry standard.
4. **Final check**: moisture levels are confirmed before any rebuild work begins.

## What should you avoid doing yourself after a sewage backup?

Handling sewage water yourself puts you in direct contact with bacteria and pathogens that a basement, laundry room or crawl space wasn't built to contain. Wading through it, running a shop vac not rated for the job, or trying to dry Cat 3-soaked drywall with box fans usually spreads contamination further into the home rather than solving it.

It's also easy to miss water that has traveled behind baseboards or under flooring, which leaves hidden contamination and sets up conditions for mold within 24 to 48 hours of a wet material staying wet, per the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home).
