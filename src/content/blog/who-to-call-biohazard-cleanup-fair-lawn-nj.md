## Police, Landlord, or Cleanup Crew: Who Handles What?

Call 911 or the police first only when the situation involves a crime, a suspicious or unattended death, or an ongoing safety threat; a biohazard cleanup crew is the right next call after that, not a janitorial service or a general handyman.

Police and the medical examiner's office document the scene and release it once their work is done. That release is usually what a cleanup crew waits for before starting work, since entering a scene before it's released can interfere with an investigation. Landlords are typically responsible for the common areas of a rental property, while the tenant or the tenant's estate is often responsible for the unit itself, though this varies by lease and by situation. A biohazard or blood cleanup call is the same call either way: both involve contaminated porous and non-porous surfaces that need PPE, EPA-registered disinfectants, and proper disposal of regulated medical waste, not a mop and a bottle of bleach.

## What Should You Avoid Touching Before Help Arrives?

Don't touch, wipe, or walk through the affected area, and don't try to disinfect it yourself with household cleaners. Bloodborne pathogens like hepatitis B, hepatitis C, and HIV can survive on surfaces for hours to days, and standard consumer cleaners aren't formulated to neutralize them the way EPA-registered, hospital-grade disinfectants are.

If it's safe to do so, ventilate the room by opening a window, close the door to the affected area, and keep pets and children out. Leave any contaminated clothing, bedding, or furniture in place rather than bagging it yourself. [OSHA's bloodborne pathogens guidance](https://www.osha.gov/bloodborne-pathogens) is written for workplaces, but the underlying precaution applies at home too: treat any blood or bodily fluid as potentially infectious until a trained crew has assessed and contained it.

## What Happens When a Biohazard Crew Arrives?

A biohazard cleanup visit starts with assessment and containment, then moves to removal, disinfection, and documentation your insurer can use. The crew isolates the affected area first, often with poly sheeting, before any cleaning begins.

From there the work generally follows this order:

1. **Assessment** of what's contaminated, including materials that look clean but have absorbed fluid, like subflooring or carpet padding.
2. **Containment** of the work area to prevent cross-contamination to the rest of the home.
3. **Removal** of non-salvageable porous materials (carpet, padding, some drywall) following state and local regulated-waste handling rules.
4. **Disinfection** of remaining surfaces with EPA-registered, hospital-grade products, following the structure of the [ANSI/IICRC S540 Standard for Trauma and Crime Scene Cleanup](https://iicrc.org/s540/).
5. **Documentation**, including photos and a written scope, for your insurance claim or the property manager's file.

A written scope before work begins is standard practice, not an upsell. Every job is different in size and contamination level, so get that scope in writing before cleanup starts.

## Will Odor Linger After the Cleanup Is Done?

Biological odors often outlast the visible stain because fluid can soak into porous materials like subfloor, grout lines, or baseboards before anyone notices. A crew that only wipes the surface can leave the smell behind.

That's why odor control is usually a distinct step after disinfection, not an afterthought. It can mean sealing a subfloor, replacing padding that can't be fully decontaminated, or running a hydroxyl generator or thermal fogger to break down odor at the molecular level rather than masking it.
