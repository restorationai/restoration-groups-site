## What Should a Flood Cleanup Crew Do in the First Hour?

In the first hour, a flood cleanup crew should stop any active water source if one exists, begin pumping or extracting standing water, and photograph the damage before anything gets moved.

After that, the crew classifies the water (clean, gray or black), checks for electrical hazards in submerged outlets and panels, and starts pulling contents off wet flooring so drying equipment can go down. Documentation starts immediately: photos, moisture readings and a written description of what was affected, since this is what your insurer will ask for later.

## Why Does IICRC Certification Matter for Flood Work?

IICRC certification means the technicians on-site are trained to the [ANSI/IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/) for water damage restoration, which sets the accepted methods for classifying water, placing drying equipment and verifying a structure is actually dry before a job closes.

Without that standard, there's no consistent way to confirm a basement is dry versus just dry-looking on the surface. An IICRC-trained crew measures moisture in the subfloor and wall cavities, not just the surface, and keeps readings on file in case mold shows up later and the insurer asks for proof of the drying process.

## Groundwater vs Clean Water Flooding: Who Handles Each?

Groundwater that seeps into a basement through hydrostatic pressure against the foundation is classified as Category 2 gray water, not clean water, while river and storm flooding like what the Passaic brings into Fair Lawn basements is Category 3 black water. A clean supply-line break is the only one of the three that starts as Category 1.

Any IICRC-certified flood crew, including The Restoration Group's, is trained and equipped to handle all three categories, but the protocol changes: Category 2 and 3 water usually means removing wet drywall, insulation and carpet rather than drying them in place, plus antimicrobial treatment and PPE for the crew. Clean water from a failed appliance line has more materials that can simply be dried and kept.

## What Should You Ask Before You Sign a Work Authorization?

Before signing anything, get clear answers on:

1. Is this a written scope of work, or an open-ended time-and-materials authorization?
2. Will the company bill your insurer directly, or do you pay and get reimbursed?
3. What happens if they find more damage once walls or flooring come out?
4. Can you cancel before work starts if a second opinion changes your mind?
5. What certifications does the crew on-site actually hold, and can they show the number?

A company that can answer all five without hesitation is one worth trusting with a basement full of water.

## How Do Flood Cleanup Companies Bill Insurance?

Most flood cleanup companies estimate the job using Xactimate-based line pricing, the same software your insurance adjuster uses, then either bill the insurer directly or bill you and supply the documentation needed for reimbursement.

One thing to confirm up front: river and storm flooding like Ida's is typically excluded from a standard homeowners policy and requires a separate NFIP or private flood policy. A burst pipe inside the home is a different claim entirely. See [What Does Flood Insurance Cover for New Jersey Homeowners](/blog/what-does-flood-insurance-cover/) and [FEMA's flood insurance overview](https://www.fema.gov/flood-insurance) before you assume either claim is automatic.

## Red Flags to Watch for When Hiring After a Flood

Watch for a company that showed up unsolicited door-to-door right after the storm, demands full payment before any work begins, won't put a scope of work in writing, or pressures you to sign a blank authorization form on the spot. Also be cautious of a crew that can't name a certification number or doesn't have a local address you can verify. A legitimate company will slow down long enough to explain the process, even when the water isn't waiting.
